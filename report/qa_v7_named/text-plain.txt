Can a small validation leaderboard rank robustness
predictors? Identifiability limits in AIMO-Interp

Jung Min Kang
Independent

Abstract
How much evidence does a small public leaderboard provide about robustness prediction? We analyze a hash-pinned AIMO-Interp leaderboard snapshot using its
observed score grid. The smallest compatible item counts are 14 for Small and 21
for Main. Under an explicit independent fair-coin item null and an independentsubmission model, we adjust each owner’s best visible score for its number of
scored submissions, then apply Benjamini–Hochberg across owners. Only 2 of
61 Small owners and 1 of 41 Main owners reject that conditional null; every surviving owner has a perfect score. Non-rejection does not establish chance performance, method equivalence, or a pairwise ranking. Exact one-sided binomial
power at true accuracy 0.6 is 0.12 and 0.2 on the respective grids. Power first
reaches 0.8 at 158 items for accuracy 0.6 and 69 for 0.65; discreteness makes the
sustained thresholds larger. We recommend at least 76–169 independent items
for those stated effects before accounting for leaderboard selection. A supporting
public-label audit finds no mixed labels among 11 Small problems with multiple
checkpoints. We provide CPU reproduction, parsing and sensitivity contracts, and
distinguish observed leaderboard statistics from unmeasured private results for our
own controls.

1

Task and the evidence a leaderboard supplies

The AIMO Interpretability Challenge asks whether a model will answer a mathematical problem
robustly under meaning-preserving perturbations. A predictor receives a problem and access to a
specified model, then produces a binary robustness decision. The Small track uses a fixed set of
open checkpoints; Main adds a larger checkpoint with multiple reasoning-effort configurations. The
scored outcome is plain accuracy on held-out problem–model instances. A leaderboard is therefore
an evaluation instrument for generalization to those instances, while an interpretability claim also
needs evidence about what signal a method uses. A high development score and a model-specific
explanation are separate claims. [1, 2, 3]
The public dataset card says that the validation set is close to balanced. An earlier public technical
report states that validation and test are balanced per problem and per model, and describes an
always-robust result on the Small validation set. We attribute that stronger balance statement to
the prior report; we do not infer exact balance from the phrase “close to balanced.” The organizer
website also says that the exact private evaluation criterion is unpublished. These sources motivate a
fair-coin reference, but they do not establish the independence assumptions required for a binomial
test. Our statistical claims below are conditional on a declared null and scoring model. [1, 2, 5]
The scientific question is whether the public validation board can separate plausible improvements
in accuracy from score variation and repeated selection. A board with few items can still detect an
extreme outcome. It can simultaneously have little power for a useful but moderate improvement,
especially when owners can try several methods and retain the best score. The relevant unit for the

selection adjustment is the owner within each track, rather than each displayed score row. Neither an
isolated significant owner nor a collection of non-significant owners is itself an ordering of methods.
Our main contribution is an executable, snapshot-specific assessment of that evidential limit. We
specify how the score grid is reconstructed, how invalid predictions and duplicate rows enter the
denominator, how an owner’s best visible result is adjusted, and what sample size detects stated
effects under the same null. We then connect this result to a supporting label audit: a training set
dominated by problem identity cannot by itself teach the contrast that a balanced per-problem test is
designed to measure. That latter observation overlaps prior public work and is not our lead novelty
claim. The report concerns evaluation design and uncertainty; it does not propose a new robustness
predictor.

2

A conditional analysis of the visible leaderboard

2.1

Snapshot, units and score reconstruction

We freeze the public private-validation leaderboard snapshot dated October 19, 2026, fetched on
October 20 in Korea. The reproduction package stores the raw JSON pages, their hashes and the
immutable analysis inputs. Snapshot date means the dated archive used for this calculation, not that
every submitted run occurred on that date. The analysis refuses to compute after a snapshot hash
mismatch. This avoids quietly replacing a published denominator when the online board changes.
[8]
The integer item counts are inferred from the score grid. For each track, the parser searches for the
smallest item count compatible with the displayed accuracy and coverage after rounding, together
with the integer invalid-prediction count. It represents a scored submission by its integer number
of correct items divided by that denominator, counting invalid predictions as wrong. The smallest
compatible counts are 14 and 21. This finite-grid assumption is explicit: the chosen unweighted
integer score model and smallest-fit rule cannot reveal undocumented weighting or resampling. The
exact item counts should ultimately be confirmed by the evaluator’s item-level scoring contract.
The parser de-duplicates repeated score rows before counting submissions. A submission scored on
both tasks contributes once to each relevant track. An observed submission has conflicting score sets;
the declared resolution uses its zero-coverage set. Zero-coverage rows remain part of the parsing
record but do not enter owner counts or the distribution of scored best results. This resolution
does not change the reported survivor counts. The analysis uses owner identifiers only to group
submissions; participant handles remain confined to the hashed raw snapshot.
After these rules, Small contains 249 scored submissions from 61 owners and Main contains 132
from 41. The units matter: a repeated row is not a new attempt, while several distinct submissions
by one owner create a selection opportunity. A method may change between submissions, so the
owner-level result describes the best visible outcome selected from that owner’s attempts. It does
not estimate the accuracy of a stable, pre-specified algorithm.

The fair-coin null is a calibration model. It is not a claim that actual submissions guess, nor a

For an owner with m scored submissions and best single-submission tail probability p, we compute the probability of observing at least one equally extreme result under independent, identically
calibrated submission scores:
padj = 1 − (1 − p)m .
This is the independent-submission selection adjustment. It grows with the number of opportunities to obtain a high score. For independent counts obeying the stated binomial null, it is exact at
attainable score thresholds; with merely valid discrete p-values it is conservative. We do not fit a
distribution of latent owner skill. The calculation is fully determined by the best score, the track
denominator and the visible scored-submission count.
We apply the Benjamini–Hochberg step-up rule separately within Small and Main at false-discoveryrate target 0.05. Sorting the adjusted p-values across the M scored owners, the rule retains the largest
rank r satisfying the threshold below and rejects the preceding ranks. Bonferroni at level 0.05 is
reported as a sensitivity check. [9]
padj,(r) ≤ q r/M.
The conventional false-discovery-rate guarantee requires valid owner p-values and suitable dependence conditions, such as independent owners. Public methods can share code, data, observations
and leaderboard feedback. We do not observe those dependencies. Bonferroni avoids the acrossowner dependence requirement when its input p-values are valid, but still inherits the binomial item
model and the independent-submission adjustment. Equal counts under the two corrections are useful sensitivity evidence within this model; they do not remove its assumptions.

A perfect score is an extreme event under this null regardless of the procedure that produced it.
Significance therefore cannot establish that the procedure used a model-internal signal, avoided
leakage, or generalizes to new problems. The snapshot records 1 perfect Small submission through
the queue named aimo_organizer_rerun. We report this as an observed queue label and make
no inference about how that run was produced or why it used that queue. The analysis assigns no
causal interpretation or accusation to a score or a queue.
Conversely, a non-rejected owner may have useful accuracy. Low power, multiplicity and a small
number of visible trials can all prevent rejection. Our survivor count is neither the number of valid
methods nor the number of methods better than random in the target population. It is a count of
visible owners separated from the stated reference under the stated analysis. The surviving owners
are also not ordered against each other by a significance test. Ranking two predictors requires their

3.3

Evaluation design implications

A useful next evaluation would preserve the problem as a grouping unit and release enough itemlevel outcomes to distinguish within-problem model contrasts from between-problem difficulty. If
several instances share one problem, adding model configurations can add less independent information than adding new problems. The item budgets above assume independent correctness indicators;
positive within-problem correlation would generally reduce effective information. A design should
state the number of problems, the number of model instances per problem, balance constraints,
invalid-prediction handling and any weighting before treating a nominal row count as its statistical
sample size.
For method comparison, a paired analysis is preferable to subtracting independent accuracy estimates, because predictors face the same instances. The evaluator can report which methods disagree
and evaluate their paired accuracy difference while preserving problem clusters. A constant prediction and a problem-only control are also informative reference methods under the actual private
scoring protocol. Their measured scores test practical behavior; they cannot reconstruct hidden label
balance from a small aggregate score. Finally, leaderboard feedback should be limited or separated
from a final holdout so that iteration does not silently consume the confirmation set.
The final-test size is unpublished in the sources we inspected. We therefore make no claim that
the eventual final ranking has the same limitations as this visible validation snapshot. A larger,
well-designed final set could resolve differences that the public board cannot. The recommendation
is to publish its effective evaluation units and uncertainty, rather than infer its adequacy from the
validation grid.

Supporting label audit and problem-only controls

4.1

Training agreement does not identify model contrasts

The pinned training table has 94 rows over 36 problems, with 80 labelled rows and 14 rows in an
unlabelled gap. The dataset card states the labelled-row rule verbatim:
is_robust = true iff max_drop <= 0.10
is_robust = false iff max_drop >= 0.25
This public rule does not specify the private-label procedure. The labelled Small subset contains 43
rows over 22 problems. Among its 11 problems represented by multiple checkpoints, 0 have mixed
binary labels. Thus the observed training labels supply no within-problem label contrast for that
repeated-checkpoint cohort. [2]
An earlier public report already identifies problem-level shortcuts and reports exact per-problem
balance in validation and test. Under exact within-problem balance, any deterministic problem-only
predictor that gives the same binary answer for all model instances of a problem has accuracy exactly
0.5 on that problem, and therefore overall under equal item scoring. This follows by counting correct
positive and negative instances, regardless of how the predictor learned its problem-level answer.
The conclusion is conditional on that balance contract and same-answer behavior; it is not a proof
that every text-based system is incapable of generalization. [5]
We retain two frozen Small bundles: a length-and-digit control and an always-robust rule. Their own
private-validation results are UNMEASURED because we have no linked owner receipt recording
the bundle, submission identifier, status, accuracy and time. The anticipated constant/control results
are receipt expectations motivated by public evidence, not pre-registered predictions of unknown
labels. Even if both receipts match those expectations, they would show consistency for those submissions rather than prove exact per-problem balance. Final-test performance of these bundles is
also UNMEASURED. This owner-result limitation is separate from our observed analysis of the
public board.

Limitations and sensitivity directions

The strongest limitation is calibration. Our binomial null assumes independent items with the same
correctness probability. Shared problems, models and perturbations can create dependence; exact
balance can itself constrain the outcome distribution of particular rules. The inferred denominators
also rely on the smallest compatible unweighted score grid. We report these assumptions at the point
of the main result because a small p-value under a convenient null is not an unconditional validation
of a predictor.
The within-owner selection adjustment assumes independent submissions. Closely related submissions are likely to be positively correlated, so they can provide fewer effective opportunities than
m independent draws. Under such positive dependence the independence adjustment can be too
large and reduce the number of rejections. Other dependence patterns can have a different direction;
negative dependence can make it too small relative to the union event. Score-adaptive development
and stopping can invalidate both a fixed-trial model and a correction using the realized number of
attempts. The union-bound sensitivity also needs a fixed family or conditional validity given the
family and counts; observing all attempts alone does not establish that condition. We cannot estimate effective attempts or stopping rules from aggregate scores. The release therefore exposes the
unadjusted tail, visible m and adjusted tail rather than hiding this choice in a single survivor count.
Only visible scored submissions are counted. Holding the observed best score fixed and retaining
the same independent-submission null, including additional hidden attempts would increase the adjusted tail and could only reduce the reported survivors. In that restricted sensitivity analysis, the
visible count is an optimistic upper bound. This is not an unconditional upper bound under unknown
dependence, missing owners, an unseen higher best score or a misspecified null. The two directions
must be read together: unobserved attempts can strengthen the selection penalty, while redundant
positively correlated attempts can weaken it.
Across-owner dependence presents an additional issue for the Benjamini–Hochberg interpretation.
Shared solutions or public discoveries can correlate owner outcomes. The matching Bonferroni
count is helpful because its family-wise bound allows arbitrary dependence across valid owner pvalues. It does not validate the within-owner p-values. We avoid describing the adjusted analysis as
a causal measurement of leaderboard overfitting or as a diagnosis of any individual submission.
The snapshot is finite and time-specific. New submissions, scoring changes, owner visibility or an
altered evaluator can change the result. The report has not measured our own frozen bundles on
private validation and has no final-test outcomes. Public-training concordance, sample metadata associations and reported participant experiences support evaluation cautions; they are not substitutes
for those missing results. A prior text-control report and a prior probe stress test discuss weaker public performance under distribution shift, which supplies context rather than a matched comparison
to our analysis. [6, 7]
7

Finally, single-method power is not ranking power. The item recommendations concern a one-sided
test against the fair-coin reference at the stated effects and target. Pairwise ranking, small accuracy
gaps, correlated outcomes, multiple comparisons and repeated validation feedback can require more
data or a different design. We have not established that a method lacking significance is equivalent
to chance, that surviving methods differ from one another, or that the public board predicts the final
ordering.

Reproduction and assistance

The release supplies a hash-pinned CPU pipeline rather than additional model runs. It verifies the
leaderboard snapshot and parquet inputs before computing; registers measured values in a claims
ledger; renders every reported measurement through checked numeric keys; and builds both author
variants from one Markdown manuscript using the unchanged official NeurIPS style. The leaderboard tool records denominator fits, parsing resolutions, owner counts, corrected tails, exact power at
the current grids and the sustained-threshold certification. The reproduction gates also test missing,
early, mismatched and bad-hash receipt fixtures without treating them as real submissions.
The archive includes immutable dataset revisions, file hashes and a downloader. It does not redistribute third-party labelled data with unresolved licence terms. A separate publication record
contains clean-clone checks, source/PDF hashes, page counts and render review. Those records establish what was executed and which artifacts can be rebuilt; compilation alone does not validate the
assumptions of the statistical model. The raw leaderboard retains source identifiers for auditability,
while the scientific report discusses aggregated owner counts and neutral source titles.
The practical result is a reproducible evidential boundary: under a declared calibration model, the
visible validation grid separates only perfect observed outcomes after selection and multiplicity, and
has low single-method power for the stated moderate effects. A larger confirmation set, item-level
paired outcomes and explicit grouping/balance rules would make the board more informative about
robustness predictors and about the interpretability claims attached to them.
